A simple way to practise

How to use Year 6 maths practice

These sets cover selected Year 6 skills. Show each step, check that a rule works for more than one example, and substitute answers back into equations to verify them.

  • For a growing pattern, compare consecutive stages and explain both what stays the same and what changes.
  • For an equation with brackets, work out the bracketed structure and use inverse operations to find an unknown; substitute it back to check.
  • When following an algorithm, record each step in order and check the decision condition before repeating or stopping.
  • Try an open challenge: start at 2; add 3 when the current number is even and 2 when it is odd; continue until the result is greater than 20. Record and explain the pattern.
  • When comparing data, use a shared scale and compare mode, range and shape. Check the sample, collection method and axis before accepting a media claim.
  • For a prism, compare a slice parallel to its base with the base shape. On a Cartesian plane, change x for left/right movement and y for down/up movement.
  • A tessellation covers a flat surface without gaps or overlaps. Test a repeated pattern with paper shapes or geometry software and describe the translations, reflections or rotations used.
  • For a rectangle, make a rows-and-columns array of equal squares. The number of rows times the number in each row gives its area in square units.
  • When multiplying a decimal by 10, 100 or 1,000, use place value to track how each digit's value changes. Estimate first and check whether the result should grow or shrink.
  • To find the median, order the values and take the middle one; with an even count, average the two middle values. The median is not dragged by one extreme value, which is why media often report median prices and wages.
  • In a mixed calculation, work out brackets first, then multiplications and divisions left to right, then additions and subtractions: 4 + 3 × 5 = 19, but (4 + 3) × 5 = 35.

Investigate a claim and report what the data supports

  1. Look at the fundraiser graph in the Year 6 data quiz. Redraw it from zero and compare how the difference between $44 and $50 appears.
  2. Choose a small, anonymous question that can be answered with relevant data. Decide who to ask, what each response means and how you will record it consistently.
  3. Use a spreadsheet or tally table to check the responses, then select a display that fits the data.
  4. Report the mode, range or shape where appropriate. State who answered and one thing the data cannot prove.

A graph can support a conclusion without proving its cause or showing what every person thinks. Never collect names for this practice activity.

Build a pattern, solve an equation and test a rule

  1. Use tiles to make growing stages with 3, 5 and 7 tiles. Predict Stage 7, describe the rule and explain how you know.
  2. Solve 4 × (□ + 2) = 36. Show each inverse step and substitute your answer to check it.
  3. Start at 2. If the current number is even, add 3; if it is odd, add 2. Repeat until the result is greater than 20. Record every result and describe the pattern.
  4. Change one instruction and predict how the sequence will change. Then test your prediction.

Use paper tiles, counters or a spreadsheet to make and test your own rules. The quiz checks selected examples; it cannot show a learner's full explanation or independently created algorithm.

Explore prisms, coordinates and tessellations

  1. Draw or build a right prism. Sketch two cuts parallel to its base and compare the cross-sections; then compare with a pyramid.
  2. Plot a point in each quadrant. Move one point left, right, up or down and record how its x- or y-coordinate changes.
  3. Use equal paper tiles or geometry software to make a repeating pattern. Combine a slide, flip or turn and check the joins for gaps and overlaps.

The quiz checks selected relationships. Building solids, plotting points independently and creating tessellations require practical or digital work as well.

Discover the rectangle area formula

Draw a rectangle on grid paper, count the squares in one row and count the rows. Make a second rectangle with different dimensions and write a multiplication that counts every square unit.

Then choose an area and one side length, and work backwards to find a possible other side. Explain why area divided by one side gives the missing side.

Test decimal place-value shifts

Choose decimals such as 0.8, 2.35 and 14.6. Before calculating, estimate each result after multiplying or dividing by 10 or 100. Record what happens to each digit and explain why placeholder zeros sometimes appear.

Check the pattern with a place-value chart, then use the inverse operation to see whether your starting value can be recovered.
